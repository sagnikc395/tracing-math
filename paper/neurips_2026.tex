\title{Decodability Is Not Localization:\
Auditing Hidden-State Error Probes in Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct}
\workshoptitle{Interpretability for Discovery}
\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{abstract}
We audit whether hidden-state probes identify when mathematical reasoning first becomes invalid, or
instead exploit easier correlates of an invalid prefix. In a case study of \model{} on \dataset{},
a linear probe predicts the persistent target \emph{invalid so far} with held-out AUROC $0.866$.
Equally tuned nuisance baselines collapse most of that margin: prefix TF--IDF reaches $0.760$,
structural metadata $0.780$, and a joint text-plus-metadata model with final-answer correctness
reaches $0.874$---numerically \emph{above} the hidden state (paired difference $-0.008$, 95\% CI
$[-0.028,0.013]$). The hidden probe's remaining value is narrower and task-specific: it localizes
the annotated onset better (Process F1 $0.393$ versus $0.294$; paired difference $0.099$
$[0.040,0.158]$) and rejects correct traces that the joint shortcut model falsely alarms on, while
it localizes exactly on only $28.9\%$ of erroneous traces and falsely alarms on $38.8\%$ of correct
traces. A post-hoc, metadata-matched transition probe obtains AUROC $0.769$ on held-out pairs;
matched-covariate balance diagnostics and reuse quantification keep it explicitly exploratory. Two
verdict readouts fail their behavioral validity checks, so the intervention remains a failed pilot.
We conclude that decodability, incremental information over tuned nuisances, localization,
calibration, and demonstrated causal use are distinct validation levels, and we extract a concrete
checklist for when an internal pattern is safe to treat as a candidate discovery.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Process supervision asks whether each step of a solution is valid, rather than whether the final
answer happens to be correct \citep{cobbe2021verifiers,lightman2023verify}. A related mechanistic
question is whether a solving model's own representation changes when its reasoning first goes
wrong. Probe accuracy alone cannot answer it: if every boundary after the first error is labeled
positive, a classifier may recognize accumulated inconsistency, solution length, generator style,
or final-answer outcome without responding at the onset.

The \emph{Interpretability for Discovery} framing makes the stakes explicit: a linearly decodable
pattern is a \emph{candidate}, not yet discovered knowledge. Turning a candidate into a claim
requires validation at distinct levels, and failing any one changes what the pattern can be used
for. This paper is a worked negative example of that validation sequence. We test four claims that
are often collapsed: (i) invalid prefixes are linearly decodable; (ii) a score localizes the first
error; (iii) the signal transfers across benchmark sources; and (iv) the model behaviorally uses
the decoded direction. We test them in one bounded setting: \model{} on \dataset{}
\citep{yang2024qwenmath,zheng2025processbench}, with problem-grouped splits and a persistent
invalid-so-far target, audited by prefix-text, metadata, and joint nuisance controls fit under the
same validation budget as the probe, source and generator stratification, an onset-aligned
analysis, and a matched transition probe.

The audit changes the interpretation. The hidden-state probe's pooled AUROC is high, but an
equally tuned joint shortcut model combining prefix text, structural metadata, and final-answer
correctness exceeds it numerically. What survives is a narrower, task-specific advantage:
trace-level localization and correct rejection, both weak in absolute terms, with poor
length- and source-dependent calibration and an uninterpretable behavioral assay. We contribute a
documented failure mode: strong prefix decoding and cross-source ranking can coexist with no
incremental pooled information over tuned nuisances.

\section{Related work and contribution boundary}

External process reward models learn step quality from human or model supervision
\citep{lightman2023verify,li2023stepaware}. \dataset{} annotates the earliest error in 3,400
model-generated traces from GSM8K, MATH, OlympiadBench, and Omni-MATH
\citep{zheng2025processbench,hendrycks2021math,he2024olympiadbench,gao2024omnimath}. We repartition
these benchmark sources for representation analysis; they are heterogeneous sources, not
independent domains or evidence of model-family generalization.

Closest to this work, Sun et al.\ probe arithmetic errors and use the probe for selective
re-prompting; ReProbe learns hidden-state step scorers for test-time scaling; and STEP uses
hidden-state scores to prune reasoning traces
\citep{sun2025arithmetic,ni2026reprobe,liang2026earlysignals}. Table~\ref{tab:delta} states the
comparison at the level needed for our claim: our novelty is not that hidden states predict
reasoning quality, but the joint audit of a human first-error target using first-crossing,
matched-transition, source-transfer, equally tuned nuisance, and failed-assay evidence.

\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Data flow, model, and split}

Each \dataset{} trace supplies a problem, generator identity, written steps, source, final-answer
correctness, and a human first-error index $e_i$, with $e_i=-1$ for fully correct traces. Traces
sharing normalized problem text form a group. Seed 42 assigns groups within source $\times$
error-status strata to 60\% train, 20\% validation, and 20\% test: of 3,400 attempted traces,
3,360 are retained (2,187 with an annotated error), yielding 24,909 written-step boundaries and
669 held-out traces. A 2,048-token limit excludes a complete trace rather than truncating it.
Appendix~\ref{app:data} gives the full attempted/retained accounting by split and source.

We run \model{} in bfloat16 and record its 1,536-dimensional residual-stream state at each written
step and each of 29 hidden-state indices. The completed extraction used batch size 16 on one
NVIDIA A100 GPU. Locked package versions, compute, storage, and licenses appear in
Appendix~\ref{app:repro}; artifact identity fixes the model, context length, dtype, and dataset
SHA-256.

\subsection{Persistent target, probe, and trace-level estimands}

At boundary $k$ of trace $i$, the frozen primary target is
\begin{equation}
y_{ik}=\mathbb{1}[e_i\geq 0\ \wedge\ k\geq e_i].
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
It means \emph{invalid so far}; it does not label only the first invalid step. A class-balanced,
L2 logistic probe is fit separately at each index after train-set standardization. Validation AUROC
selects $C\in\{0.01,0.1,1,10\}$; validation Process F1 selects a threshold on a 0.005 grid from
0.05 to 0.95 and then the layer. The selected index is 23, $C=0.01$, threshold $0.645$; it is refit
on train plus validation and evaluated once on test.

The predicted first error is the first threshold crossing, or $-1$ if none occurs. We report
boundary AUROC and average precision, exact error-boundary accuracy among erroneous traces,
correct-trace rejection, their harmonic mean (Process F1), complete-trace accuracy, and detection
within one or two steps; the localization metrics weight traces equally. Primary intervals use
1,000 whole-trace bootstrap draws; the post-hoc paired comparisons use 2,000 draws because they
were added in a later audit configuration, which we state rather than presenting one frozen
protocol.

\subsection{Shortcut, joint-control, and weighting audits}
\label{sec:controls}

Every nuisance control receives the same model-selection budget as the hidden-state probe: L2
regularization is selected from $\{0.01,0.1,1,10\}$ by validation AUROC, the threshold is selected
on the same validation scores, and the selected model is refit on train plus validation before the
single test evaluation. Each control is also fit with sample weights inverse to boundary counts,
so every trace contributes equal total training loss, mirroring a trace-equal objective rather
than reweighting evaluation after fitting. Current-step TF--IDF uses the destination step; prefix
TF--IDF uses the problem and all reasoning text through the current boundary; structural metadata
uses source, generator, absolute and fractional step position, trace length, and token count. Two
\emph{joint} baselines combine prefix text, all structural metadata, and---in the stronger
variant---final-answer correctness, so complementarity across separately fit controls cannot be
mistaken for a hidden-state advantage.

Final-answer correctness is a benchmark annotation, not an online signal: it records whether the
trace's final answer matches the benchmark reference solution, for every trace regardless of where
an error occurs. We use it as a diagnostic upper bound on nuisance predictability, not a deployable
baseline. A shuffled-label hidden probe checks the fitting procedure. We report paired
probe-minus-control intervals from whole-trace resampling, pooled and within
final-answer-correctness strata. The primary objective weights each boundary equally in
\emph{evaluation}; the trace-equal refit above addresses the training asymmetry. Descriptive
subgroup outcomes stratify by source, generator, final-answer correctness, error position, and
length and token-count quartiles.

\subsection{Post-hoc onset analyses}

The following analyses were designed after the primary test result and are labeled post-hoc. We
circularly shift each frozen score trajectory 5,000 times, preserving its values while breaking
alignment to the annotation. We compare the score jump at each noninitial error with a transition
from a fully correct trace, preferring the same source and generator and then minimizing
differences in relative position, trace length, and token count.

The transition probe is a separate analysis of $h_{i,e_i}-h_{i,e_i-1}$. Every eligible erroneous
transition is paired with a correct-trace transition in the same frozen partition, giving 1,152
train, 387 validation, and 380 test pairs; the pairing reuses correct traces (median 2 pairs per
placebo trace, 90th percentile 6, maximum 46, from 687 unique placebos), and matched groups are
close on relative position but retain token-count and length differences (standardized
differences $0.28$ and $0.15$). Layer and $C$ are selected by validation AUROC; index 21 and
$C=0.01$ are selected. Controls predict from position, destination-step TF--IDF, or shuffled
labels. The protocol is fully specified and executable, but the test set was not untouched when
the analysis was conceived, so the result is exploratory regardless of its interval.

\section{Results}

\subsection{A tuned joint shortcut model matches or exceeds pooled AUROC}

The selected probe reaches AUROC $0.866$ (95\% CI $[0.849,0.884]$), average precision $0.843$
($[0.813,0.870]$), and step F1 $0.743$ ($[0.716,0.769]$). Within erroneous traces, first-step
centering leaves pooled AUROC at $0.881$ ($[0.862,0.900]$), and mean per-trace AUROC is $0.968$
($[0.958,0.976]$). Stable trace offsets therefore do not explain all discrimination.

Table~\ref{tab:controls} shows the equally tuned comparison. Prefix text reaches AUROC $0.760$,
structural metadata $0.780$, and their joint model $0.810$: the controls' errors are partly
complementary. Adding final-answer correctness yields the strongest nuisance model at AUROC
$0.874$, numerically above the hidden probe's $0.866$ (paired hidden-minus-joint difference
$-0.008$ $[-0.028,0.013]$, which includes zero). Under a maximally fair tuning budget, the hidden
state shows \emph{no incremental pooled-ranking information} over the best nuisance predictor.

The decomposition matters more than the pooled number. The hidden probe retains a Process F1
advantage of $0.099$ ($[0.040,0.158]$) and an exact-localization advantage of $0.113$
($[0.064,0.163]$), because the joint model crosses threshold on most correct traces (correct
rejection $0.612$ versus $0.895$; hidden-minus-joint difference $-0.283$ $[-0.347,-0.218]$). What
the hidden state contributes is temporal specificity---when to start alarming---not better
separation of invalid prefixes in aggregate. Within final-answer-correctness strata, the hidden
AUROC advantage is confined to traces whose final answer is correct ($0.073$ $[0.039,0.107]$) and
vanishes among incorrect-outcome traces ($-0.001$ $[-0.022,0.021]$), consistent with the outcome
field carrying most pooled information when it is informative.

\subsection{First-error localization remains weak}

The hidden score localizes $28.9\%$ of erroneous traces exactly, $59.3\%$ within one step, and
$72.7\%$ within two. It crosses somewhere on $88.7\%$ of erroneous traces, but also on $38.8\%$
of fully correct traces, and among detections it is late by $+0.59$ steps on average
($[+0.36,+0.80]$). Process F1 is $0.393$ ($[0.351,0.436]$); complete-trace accuracy is $0.404$.

Post-hoc temporal tests show more alignment than these threshold metrics alone suggest. Exact
localization is $0.289$ versus $0.164$ under circular shifts ($p=0.0002$). Across 380 noninitial
errors, the mean onset jump is $0.257$; matched correct transitions rise $0.113$, for a paired
difference of $0.144$ ($[0.096,0.193]$). The score continues from $0.341$ immediately before the
error to $0.559$ at onset, $0.680$ one step later, and $0.731$ two steps later: a gradual
transition into a persistent state, not a sharp alarm. The transition task excludes 52 test traces
whose first error occurs at step 0, which may differ systematically from the 380 eligible ones.

The matched transition probe reaches AUROC $0.769$ ($[0.713,0.820]$), average precision $0.741$
($[0.672,0.816]$), and paired accuracy $0.753$ ($[0.676,0.824]$); position, destination-step
TF--IDF, and shuffled-label controls reach $0.63$, $0.67$, and $0.58$. Given the placebo reuse and
retained covariate imbalance documented above, this supports decodability of an onset-related
change on the already-inspected split; sensitivity refits under one-to-one and inverse-reuse
matching, and replication on untouched data, are needed before any confirmatory reading.

\subsection{Source ranking transfers; model generalization is untested}

Separate probes trained on one ProcessBench source and tested on another have mean off-diagonal
AUROC $0.805$ versus $0.845$ on the diagonal; every off-diagonal cell exceeds $0.739$. Thresholded
Process F1 is less stable: $0.261$ off diagonal versus $0.336$ on diagonal, with individual
off-diagonal values from $0.055$ to $0.371$. These cells show cross-source ranking within one
benchmark construction, not transfer to another model size, family, or dataset.

\subsection{Scope of the evidence}

This paper is an existence proof of a failure mode in one small math-tuned model on one benchmark
construction---decodability without incremental pooled information, localization, calibration, or
causal validation---not a measurement of its prevalence across models. A second-model replication
under the frozen protocols reported here is deliberately left as follow-up work.

\section{Limitations, responsible use, and conclusion}

This is a one-model, one-benchmark case study; the title and claims name \model{}. All added
analyses---transition probe, temporal randomization, shortcut and joint controls, trace-equal
refits, subgroups---are post-hoc, and all intervals are conditional on one frozen split and the
completed selection pipeline: they capture resampling uncertainty, not split or selection
sensitivity. No dated preregistration exists. Corrected/error twins lack independent verification,
so no patching result is reported; a stronger follow-up would verify twins, reserve an untouched
onset set, and validate the readout first.

The detector is unsuitable for grading, certification, or unattended feedback: its $38.8\%$
false-alarm rate on correct traces could penalize valid work, its late detections could let an
invalid derivation propagate, and its sensitivity to source, outcome, and length makes
distribution shift especially risky. Positive uses are limited to research diagnostics with human
or formal verification.

The central empirical finding is a gap with a precise shape: in \model{}, invalid-so-far prefixes
are linearly decodable, but an equally tuned joint text-plus-metadata model with final-answer
correctness matches or exceeds the hidden state's pooled AUROC; what remains is a
localization and correct-rejection advantage, itself weak in absolute terms.

For discovery practice, this audit reduces to a checklist. Before treating a decodable internal
pattern as discovered knowledge, test and report: (1) whether equally tuned nuisance models---fit
jointly, under the same selection budget---recover the pooled performance; (2) whether any residual
advantage survives on the task-specific metrics that matter, with paired intervals; (3) whether
temporal correspondence holds against matched and shifted controls; (4) whether calibration and
subgroup behavior survive the shifts the use case implies; and (5) whether a behavioral readout
distinguishes the relevant states before any causal claim. Our candidate passed (1) only in the
negative and failed the rest in absolute terms.

\section{The failed behavioral pilot}
\label{app:causal}

The original \texttt{CORRECT}/\texttt{INCORRECT} readout has baseline AUROC $0.283$ and specificity
zero. A revised question with conditional Yes/No probabilities remains below chance (AUROC $0.342$,
specificity $0.203$ on 256 balanced boundaries). The intervention code requires both AUROC
$>0.5$ and nonzero specificity before a learned-direction effect is interpreted. Neither readout
passes. Random orthogonal directions cannot substitute for a positive control of assay
sensitivity, so the dose response is not causal evidence for or against behavioral use. We report
the pilot only to delimit the evidence: no directional intervention was interpreted, so the causal
section of the audit is empty by design rather than by null result.
