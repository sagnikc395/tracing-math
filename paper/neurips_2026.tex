\begin{abstract}
We ask whether a math-specialized model's hidden states register the first invalid step.  At every
retained step boundary in the 3,400-trace ProcessBench dataset, we record the residual-stream state
of \model{} and train layer-wise linear probes to predict whether the solution is invalid so far.
With problem-grouped splits and validation-only model selection, the selected probe obtains
held-out AUROC $0.866$ (95\% whole-trace bootstrap CI
$[0.849,0.884]$), compared with $0.733$ for current-step TF--IDF and $0.730$ for position.
The same score reaches AUROC $0.873$ when restricted to erroneous traces, and source-specific
directions transfer across every pair of four mathematical domains (mean off-diagonal AUROC
$0.805$).  Temporal localization is harder: the probe identifies the exact first error
on $28.9\%$ of erroneous traces, lands within one step on $59.3\%$, and is systematically late by
$0.59$ steps among detections.  Finally, adding or subtracting the probe direction at the boundary
produces no detected change in the model's explicit correctness verdict.  Because that native
verdict readout is itself degenerate (AUROC $0.283$), the intervention is inconclusive rather than evidence of causal
non-use.  The hidden states contain a transferable invalidity signal, but the experiment does not
show precise timing or behavioral use.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A solution can reach a wrong answer through one early mistake, or reach the right answer through an
invalid derivation. Outcome accuracy hides this distinction, and verifiers increasingly score
intermediate steps rather than only final answers
\citep{cobbe2021verifiers,lightman2023verify}.  Yet an external verifier leaves open a mechanistic
question: when a solution first becomes invalid, does the solving model's own representation change
in a detectable way?

An internal signal near the first invalid step would show that the model tracks errors during an
extended computation, and it might support lightweight detection.  Three claims have to be kept
apart.  A state may contain decodable information without localizing the transition; a direction
may rank examples across domains without transferring its threshold; and the model's output
computation need not use a decodable direction
\citep{hewitt2019control,elazar2021amnesic}.

We study these distinctions in \model{} \citep{yang2024qwenmath} on \dataset{}, whose human
annotations identify the first erroneous step in solutions drawn from GSM8K, MATH,
OlympiadBench, and Omni-MATH \citep{zheng2025processbench}.  Our experiment was specified before
test results were inspected.  We extract one residual-stream state per layer and written step, fit
regularized linear probes on problem-grouped splits, evaluate first-threshold-crossing localization,
test source-to-source transfer, and intervene along the learned direction.

Our main findings are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Invalid-so-far is linearly decodable at the selected hidden-state index with AUROC $0.866$,
  exceeding position, current-step lexical, and shuffled-label controls.  Discrimination remains
  high within erroneous traces (AUROC $0.873$).
  \item The ranking signal transfers across all 12 off-diagonal source pairs, but exact localization
  is weaker and less calibrated: only $28.9\%$ of erroneous traces are localized exactly.
\item The preregistered additive intervention yields no signed dose response.  However, the
underlying explicit verdict score fails to distinguish valid from invalid boundaries, so this
result cannot say whether the decoded signal is causally used.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Process-level mathematical verification.}
Sampling solutions and ranking them with a verifier improved GSM8K performance before modern
process supervision \citep{cobbe2021verifiers}.  PRM800K then enabled direct comparison of outcome
and human step-level supervision; process supervision performed better on a representative MATH
subset \citep{lightman2023verify}.  Later step-aware verifiers and process reward models used
intermediate correctness for search and training \citep{li2023stepaware}.  These reward models can
generalize poorly from GSM8K and MATH to harder competition problems.  ProcessBench addresses this
problem by annotating the earliest error in
3,400 traces spanning four sources and evaluating both reward and prompted critic models
\citep{zheng2025processbench}.  Prompted LLM studies likewise find that identifying and correcting
mathematical errors is a separate capability from solving, and that exact first-error localization
remains difficult even when a reference solution is provided
\citep{li2024erroridentification,srivatsa2025cannotspot}.

We use ProcessBench differently from its standard benchmark setting.  We repartition it to analyze
one model's representations, so our metrics are not leaderboard scores.  Rather than score generic
step quality, we ask whether a prefix has crossed the human-annotated first-error boundary and when
the probe first detects that change.

\paragraph{Internal correctness and reasoning signals.}
Models can sometimes estimate whether their own answers are correct
\citep{kadavath2022know}, and truth-related information can be recovered from activations even when
outputs are unreliable \citep{burns2022latent}.  Work closest to ours shows that lightweight probes
detect arithmetic errors and can guide selective re-prompting \citep{sun2025arithmetic}; ReProbe
uses learned hidden-state scorers for multi-domain step verification \citep{ni2026reprobe}; and STEP
uses hidden states to prune low-quality reasoning traces during test-time scaling
\citep{liang2026earlysignals}.  Circuit analysis of simple arithmetic further finds a dissociation
between computation and validation, with smaller models relying heavily on surface-level numerical
consistency heads \citep{bertolazzi2025validationgap}.

These studies show that internal states can predict reasoning quality.  We use human first-error
labels for open-ended model-generated solutions from grade-school through Olympiad mathematics.
We measure the first score crossing rather than only step classification, test every
source-to-source transfer, and evaluate the probe direction with a held-out causal intervention.

\paragraph{From probing to causal evidence.}
Probe accuracy alone is ambiguous because a classifier may exploit accessible but behaviorally
unused information; controls and constrained probe families help but do not establish use
\citep{hewitt2019control,belinkov2022probing}.  Causal work instead modifies representations through
amnesic removal \citep{elazar2021amnesic}, causal tracing \citep{meng2022rome}, activation addition,
or inference-time steering \citep{turner2023actadd,li2023iti}.  We therefore treat intervention as
a stronger, separate test.  A null intervention is interpretable only when its output assay is
sensitive and meaningful.  Our original verdict prompt fails that condition.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Data, model, and leakage-resistant splits}

ProcessBench contains 3,400 model-generated solutions with a human label $e_i$: the zero-indexed
first erroneous step, or $-1$ if every step is correct
\citep{zheng2025processbench}.  Its four sources span grade-school word problems, competition
mathematics, and Olympiad problems
\citep{cobbe2021verifiers,hendrycks2021math,he2024olympiadbench,gao2024omnimath}.
We evaluate the 1.5B-parameter instruction-tuned Qwen2.5-Math model in bfloat16 with a 2,048-token
context limit; over-length traces are excluded rather than truncated.

All traces sharing whitespace-normalized, case-folded problem text form one group.  Within
source $\times$ error-status strata, a deterministic seed-42 allocation places approximately
60\%/20\%/20\% of groups in train/validation/test.  All steps from a problem therefore remain in one
partition.  The published test artifact contains 669 traces (432 erroneous and 237 fully correct)
and 4,985 step boundaries.

\subsection{Boundary representations and targets}

The prompt renders each step $k$ followed by a unique \texttt{END\_STEP} marker.  One causal forward
pass records the residual-stream state $h_{ik}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{1536}$ at each marker across
29 hidden-state indices.  Although later steps occur in the same prompt, causal attention prevents
them from influencing an earlier boundary state.  For trace $i$ and step $k$, the primary target is
\begin{equation}
  y_{ik}=\mathbb{1}[e_i\geq 0 \ \wedge\ k\geq e_i],
  \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
which denotes that the displayed reasoning is \emph{invalid so far}.  It is intentionally persistent
after onset and should not be read as a label of the current step in isolation.

\subsection{Predictive, localization, and transfer tests}

At each index $\ell$, we standardize dimensions using training data and fit a class-balanced,
L2-regularized logistic probe.  Validation AUROC selects
$C\in\{0.01,0.1,1,10\}$; validation Process F1 selects a threshold from 0.05 to 0.95; and validation
Process F1 (then AUROC) selects the layer.  The chosen probe is refit on train+validation and
evaluated once on test.  The selected setting is index 23, $C=0.01$, threshold $0.645$.

For a trace, the predicted first error is the first score at or above this threshold, or $-1$ if
none crosses.  We report error-trace exact accuracy, correct-trace rejection accuracy, their
harmonic mean (Process F1), complete-trace exact outcome, and tolerance-based localization.  We
also report step AUROC within erroneous traces, preventing success from a constant distinction
between wholly correct and erroneous solutions.  Controls use absolute/fractional position,
current-step TF--IDF unigrams and bigrams, and shuffled training labels.  Uncertainty uses 1,000
percentile bootstrap resamples of whole traces.

For transfer, a separate index-23 probe is trained on each source and evaluated on every test
source.  Its threshold is fixed using only the training source's validation set.  AUROC therefore
tests ranking transfer, whereas Process F1 additionally tests probability and threshold transfer.

\subsection{Probe-direction intervention}

The standardized probe weight is transformed back to raw activation coordinates and normalized to
$v$.  At the input to decoder layer 23, we replace one held-out boundary activation with
\begin{equation}
  h'=h+\alpha\sigma_v v, \qquad
  \alpha\in\{-4,-2,-1,0,1,2,4\},
  \label{eq:intervention}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_v$ is the train+validation standard deviation of projections onto $v$.  We sample one
boundary from each of 256 test traces, balanced between valid-so-far and invalid-so-far.  The
outcome is the difference between length-normalized teacher-forced log probabilities of
\texttt{INCORRECT} and \texttt{CORRECT}.  Twenty matched random unit directions orthogonal to $v$
serve as controls at $\alpha=\pm4$.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Invalidity is decodable, but first-error timing is harder}

Figure~\ref{fig:predictive} shows a rapid rise in decoding performance after the embedding state and
stable AUROC above $0.82$ through most layers.  The validation-selected index obtains test AUROC
$0.866$ (95\% CI $[0.849,0.884]$), average precision $0.843$ ($[0.813,0.870]$), and step F1 $0.743$
($[0.716,0.769]$).  Restricted to erroneous traces, it distinguishes pre-error from error-and-later
steps with AUROC $0.873$ and average precision $0.925$.

The hidden-state score exceeds current-step TF--IDF (AUROC $0.733$, Process F1 $0.254$), position
($0.730$, $0.054$), and the shuffled-label hidden-state control ($0.518$, $0.179$).  These gaps argue
against simple position and current-step lexical explanations but do not exclude them; the present
artifacts lack paired confidence intervals for probe-minus-control differences.

Exact timing is substantially weaker.  Across erroneous traces, $28.9\%$ are localized exactly,
$59.3\%$ within one step, and $72.7\%$ within two.  The detector crosses somewhere on $88.7\%$ of
erroneous traces but falsely crosses on $38.8\%$ of fully correct traces.  Complete-trace exact
accuracy is $0.404$; Process F1 is $0.393$ (95\% CI $[0.351,0.436]$).  Among detected erroneous
traces, the mean predicted-minus-human error is $+0.59$ steps (95\% CI $[+0.36,+0.80]$): the signal
is systematically late rather than a sharp internal alarm at the annotated boundary.

\subsection{Ranking transfers better than localization thresholds}

Every off-diagonal transfer AUROC in Figure~\ref{fig:transfer} exceeds $0.739$.  Mean off-diagonal
AUROC is $0.805$, compared with $0.845$ on the diagonal.  The sources therefore share part of the
ranking signal.  Thresholded localization is less stable: mean off-diagonal
Process F1 is $0.261$ versus $0.336$ on the diagonal, with individual transfers ranging from
$0.055$ to $0.371$.  Thus a source-trained direction often orders target-domain boundaries well
without transferring calibrated probabilities or the optimal first-crossing threshold.

\subsection{The causal assay is inconclusive}

Equation~\ref{eq:intervention} produces neither the predicted signed monotonic response nor a
nonzero effect at any dose.  The mean changes at $\alpha=-4$ and $+4$ are $+0.00040$
($[-0.00108,0.00185]$) and $-0.00011$ ($[-0.00169,0.00138]$), respectively.  Neither extreme beats
the distribution of 20 random orthogonal directions.

This null cannot support causal non-use because the unmodified output assay is not informative.
All 256 baseline margins favor \texttt{INCORRECT}; recall is 1, specificity is 0, and AUROC is
$0.283$.  Valid boundaries even have a larger mean incorrect-minus-correct margin (5.860) than
invalid boundaries (5.625).  We can conclude only that this additive intervention, at index 23 and
the marked boundary, did not detectably affect this degenerate teacher-forced verdict score.

\section{Discussion and limitations}The probe separates prefixes around human-annotated errors and transfers in ranking, but it often
fires late and is not shown to drive a correctness judgment.  A post-hoc analysis of the frozen
test scores found $28.9\%$ exact localization versus $16.4\%$ after circular within-trace shifts
($p=0.0002$).  The score rose by $0.257$ at the annotated onset; matched transitions in correct
traces rose by $0.113$, a difference of $0.144$ (95\% CI $[0.096,0.193]$).  First-step centering
left pooled within-error-trace AUROC at $0.881$.  Two further post-hoc tests refine this picture.
A probe trained on matched boundary-to-boundary transitions distinguishes error onsets from
metadata-matched correct-trace transitions at AUROC $0.769$ (CI $[0.713,0.820]$), above the
position ($0.63$), TF--IDF ($0.67$), and shuffled ($0.58$) controls, so a representation change at
the onset itself is linearly decodable.  Re-probing the natural last token of each step instead of
the artificial marker reproduces the pipeline at AUROC $0.868$ versus $0.866$ (paired difference
$+0.002$, CI $[-0.007,+0.011]$), so the signal does not depend on the step marker.  These checks
support a gradual temporal change, not a precise alarm, and remain post-hoc.

The score may still encode generator identity, trace-wide difficulty, final-answer outcome, or
features beyond TF--IDF.  The persistent target may reward accumulated corruption or create a
summary state; the marker-dependence concern is addressed by the boundary control above, but the
other shortcut channels remain untested.  We test one model and split at one token location, layer,
and intervention.  The compact release lacks retained train/validation and exclusion counts.
Paired bootstrap intervals over 669 traces (2,000 resamples) show the probe's AUROC edge over the
current-step TF--IDF control at $+0.133$ (CI $[0.111,0.155]$) and over the position control at
$+0.136$ (CI $[0.113,0.157]$); cross-model transfer cells remain unpaired.  A planned counterfactual
test would splice the boundary state of an erroneous prefix into a corrected twin and ask whether
the model's verdict flips; correction drafts for all 160 pairs exist but 25 await verification, so
the run and the causal question it would settle stay open.

\paragraph{Responsible use.}

This study uses no human-subject data and deploys no verifier.  False alarms, delayed detection,
distribution shift, and adversarial traces make the probe unsuitable for high-stakes grading or
certification.  Consequential calculations still require human or formal verification.
